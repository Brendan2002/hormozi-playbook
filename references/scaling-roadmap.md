# Scaling Roadmap reference

Load this only in Scale diagnosis mode. Paraphrase; don't quote.

Hormozi's $100M Scaling Roadmap places a business on a 0-9 ladder, mostly by headcount. Each stage has one main constraint, and you graduate by fixing it.

Ladder: 0 Improvise · 1 Monetize · 2 Advertise · 3 Stabilize · 4 Prioritize · 5 Productize · 6 Optimize · 7 Categorize · 8 Specialize · 9 Capitalize.

## Verified: Stages 6-8
Source: the Stage 7 personalized guide Brendan downloaded from Acquisition.com (26 pages), which covers stages 6-8 only.

- **6 Optimize** (about 20-49 people; role: leader, with managers under them). Constraint: adding people made things less efficient. Typical symptoms: improving two products at once slows both; ads work worse on colder audiences and acquisition cost rises; the core offer closes worse; churn climbs as products multiply; staff turnover puts data and access at risk; growth spending isn't producing growth. Graduate by doing better instead of more: train current people instead of hiring, improve current products instead of adding, serve current customers instead of chasing new ones, use current tools better. Typical fixes: a set process for improving products bit by bit; a way to produce ads at scale; real sales training; group customers by when they joined and how they use the product, and track success metrics per group; basic security, password protection, and tech onboarding/offboarding; detailed financials, a real balance sheet, quarterly taxes. Method: find what isn't working, find why, design a better way, test it, train people, monitor results. One measured improvement at a time; document it and make it stick.
- **7 Categorize** (about 50-99; role: executive, three layers of leadership). Constraint: every system is overloaded and the business feels disorganized. Graduate by triage: sort leads, customers, applicants, money, data and employees into categories, each with its own system. Typical fixes: assign specific teams to specific product fixes, one problem at a time; add a second acquisition channel (such as referrals) and score leads; give sales coverage schedules and a follow-up checklist, and reward reps whose leads show up to meetings; move customer service from firefighting to prevention, renewals and expansion; centralize data in the cloud; use applicant tracking and outside recruiting firms; put employee records in one HR system; set quarterly department budgets, spending limits and approvals for big purchases, plus better insurance and forecasting.
- **8 Specialize** (about 100-249; role: president, four layers of management). Constraint: no generalist can do everything well. Graduate by building dedicated specialist teams. Typical fixes: survey customers on which features they use, cut the rest, and consider selling cut features as separate products; shift toward brand-building (one clear simple message, free value that earns trust, association with trusted brands or people); route best leads to best closers and track results automatically; give multi-product customers one clear path and smart recommendations; give each department its own software plus an IT request system with clear scope; one standard hiring process, with the founder doing a final check on key hires; group onboarding training, performance management, liability insurance and constant reinforcement of values; renegotiate vendors, keep spare cash in interest-bearing accounts, use finance and tax specialists, run internal audits.

## Verified: Stages 0-5
Sources: Acquisition.com personalized scaling guides, downloaded 2026-11-04 from `acquisition.com/hubfs/0-100M Scaling Course PDFs (v6)/`. Each guide covers the stage before and after the one it is named for, so Stages 0-2 come from `Your_ACQ_100MScalingRoadmap-Stage01_.pdf` and Stages 3-5 from `...-Stage04_.pdf`. The matching sections in the Stage 0, 2, 3 and 5 guides were spot-checked and agree on role, headcount, bottom line and graduate-by. Page numbers are the PDF's printed page numbers. The guides are written in simple analogies; the entries below paraphrase them.

- **0 Improvise** (headcount N/A in the guide; official ladder page says 0-1; role: researcher). Bottom line: nothing is happening yet. Graduate by getting people to try the offer for free and like it, with people using it and coming back. Typical fixes: make something you can give away free (service, information or product); tell people you already know; ask each tester what to improve; look after free users as if they paid; keep tools basic; set up a business entity such as an LLC, a business bank account, and expense tracking. Don't worry about revenue yet; keep spending low. Source: Stage01 guide pp. 16-20.
- **1 Monetize** (headcount 1; official ladder page 0-1; role: starter). Constraint: the business makes no money; the product isn't yet good enough for people to pay. Graduate by making the first sale, then consistent first sales to different customers, with people willing to pay your prices, tracking of money, and basic systems. Typical symptoms: nervous about charging, worried nobody will pay, free-to-paid shift. Typical fixes: build a "Version 1" product that is good enough to sell, not perfect; tell people it is now paid and why it is worth it; use free customers' results as proof and start with people who liked the free version; expect paying customers to demand more and look for hidden costs that annoy them; use free tools and forums; sign up for free ad credits; get part-time help from freelancers on Upwork or Fiverr; set up a payment tool (Square or PayPal) and run a real payment; write basic agreements and get things in writing. Source: Stage01 guide pp. 5-12.
- **2 Advertise** (headcount 1; official ladder page 0-1; role: doer, with freelancers or part-timers). Constraint: new customers are inconsistent. Graduate by letting more people know about the business consistently: quality is consistent, new customers come regularly, marketing is steady, money is organized, help is reliable, systems work. Typical symptoms: busy some weeks, quiet others; more complaints from paying customers; helper turnover; feeling constantly behind. Typical fixes: fix product reliability in an "unscalable" way, doing whatever keeps customers happy; follow the "Rule of 100" (the guide says 100 minutes of marketing a day) and market daily, not only when slow; track which actions bring customers; double down on what works instead of diversifying; write a consistent sales script, learn the CLOSER method and practise the pitch; handle upset customers with the "Angry Boat" method and collect testimonials with the "Epiphany Script"; set up a simple CRM and a basic website; convert part-timers to full-time, post job ads, set up W-2/1099 paperwork, training and schedules; use accounting software, save for taxes, check the bank account daily. Source: Stage01 guide pp. 23-30.
- **3 Stabilize** (headcount 1-4; role: trainer; the guide says about 24% of businesses, around 7 million, reach it). Constraint: too much work for one person. Graduate by getting help and building a small team that can run the business. Typical symptoms: chaos; you can't fix everything customers complain about; leads buy slower because they don't know you; time wasted on unserious prospects; new customers feel lost; too many unused software tools; hard to hire; informal pay and mishandled employee taxes. Typical fixes: fix the single biggest customer complaint first, then the next; send emails and content to build trust over time; define what makes a good prospect; create a welcome and onboarding path for new customers; cancel unused tools and negotiate trials or discounts on the rest; track a basic sales pipeline; write job descriptions and screen resumes properly; use a payroll service; basic bookkeeping and invoice management; document important tasks, train, equip and check the team's work. Source: Stage04 guide pp. 13-17.
- **4 Prioritize** (headcount 5-9; role: manager, one layer between owner and front line; the guide says about 3 million businesses reach it). Constraint: trying to be everything to everyone; saying yes to any paying customer pulls the product in too many directions. Graduate by niching down to serve only people like your best customers. Typical symptoms: too many unqualified leads; slow lead follow-up; no way to measure customer happiness; scattered customer information; team using tools inconsistently; time wasted interviewing poor fits; no employee policies; surprised by "one-time" expenses. Typical fixes: choose an ideal customer and update the product for them, possibly raising prices; say no to poor fits; better free content to attract the right people; track calls made, leads to sales and response time to leads; measure customer satisfaction; put customer notes in one CRM; standardize tools, protect passwords and data, add project management and team chat; learn to interview and check references; write an employee handbook; produce proper profit and loss statements; get basic business insurance. Source: Stage04 guide pp. 5-9.
- **5 Productize** (headcount 10-19; role: director, with the first 2-3 managers under you; the guide says about 2.4 million businesses reach it). Constraint: not making enough per customer (low LTV:CAC); after the first purchase customers have nothing else to buy. Graduate by making and selling a second premium product to existing customers. Typical symptoms: qualified leads getting expensive; salespeople overpromising, causing refunds and bad reviews; inconsistent customer support; employees on personal computers and software, with company data leaving when they do; hard to find managers; terminating people incorrectly; not knowing how much can be reinvested. Typical fixes: connect customer service to product development and ask what else customers want; start the second product small and test it with best customers, priced often higher than the first; keep the first product running smoothly; create sales materials and record customer calls for training; target marketing more tightly; write a customer service playbook and a path to higher-level products; company-owned equipment and software, lead and customer tracking, dashboards; promote from within, set termination policies and encourage employee referrals; build a real budget, simple forecasting, and track tax write-offs. Source: Stage04 guide pp. 20-24.

## How to apply it
- Check headcount and the main constraint before proposing a stage. If either is missing or they conflict, label placement provisional or unknown and ask. Do not infer company stage from street-team size. Advise for the current situation, not a bigger business.
- Moozy: Brendan coordinates street-team/events work and has sales responsibilities; do not assume company-wide decision authority. Apply stage advice to his own scope (his leads, event staff, sampling budget, data) and frame company-wide items as recommendations to Seth.
- His side ventures are early-stage (likely Stages 0-2); don't push Stage 6-8 systems onto them. Stages 0-2 overlap at 0-1 people, so decide among them by constraint, not headcount.
- Most portable items for his street-team scope: lead scoring and a second lead source, follow-up checklists, rewarding show-ups, and grouping store partners by start date to spot drop-off.
- Running theme: better beats more. Before adding a channel, hire, product or tool, check whether improving the current one wins.
